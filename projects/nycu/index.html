Decoding bits that moved

May 2026 to Jul 2026 · Hsinchu, Taiwan

Classical error correction assumes every bit stays in its slot: bit 37 arrives as bit 37, possibly flipped. Delete one bit and every bit after it shifts, so a decoder comparing position by position sees dozens of errors where there was one. I benchmarked machine learning decoders against that channel, and the most useful thing I built was the measurement that showed how bad it is.

Break a codeword

Send a random codeword through a channel that inserts, deletes and flips bits. Then score what arrived two ways. Position by position, the way a conventional bit error rate is computed, every bit after the first insertion or deletion is compared against the wrong neighbor. By alignment, the way an edit distance is computed, the received word is lined up against the sent one and only the actual edits count.

Green plus is an insertion, red minus a deletion, amber a flipped bit. Run a thousand trials to see the averages at these settings.

On the project's dataset of one million sequences, the sent words were 100 bits and the received words ran from 89 to 115. Only about 13% kept their length at all. Among the pairs that did, position scoring reported about 30 mismatches for a true edit distance of about 8. That gap is the whole argument for alignment based losses over positional ones, and it is why the models below were pointed at edit distance rather than at bits.

Watch the edit distance table fill

Edit distance is computed with a table: cell (i, j) holds the cheapest way to turn the first i bits of one word into the first j bits of the other, built from its three neighbors. Click the bits to change them and watch the table fill and the alignment trace back from the corner.

Three models, in the order they were tried

Autoregressive encoder decoderphase oneGenerates the decoded bits one at a time. Produces a sequence you can score every way, and on a shifting channel a positional loss drowns. Pearson 0.8589 and Spearman 0.8557 against true edit distance, BER 0.3716.
Siamese encoderphase twoLearns a 256 dimensional space in which Euclidean distance between two embedded words tracks their edit distance. Trained with triplet loss. RMSE 2.092 and MAE 1.670, the lowest error of any learned model tested, beating both alignment baselines. Gives distances, not bits.
CTC headphase threeMeant to bridge the two: decode bits directly with a loss that is indifferent to alignment. Did not converge. Written up as a diagnosed failure with a named fix, below.

The honest footnote on the Siamese result: its rank correlation fell to roughly 0.45, below the convolutional and alignment baselines. Triplet loss pushes each pair past a fixed margin, which tightens local ordering and absolute error without ever asking the whole severity scale to line up in rank. Good distances, weaker ranking. Knowing why is worth more than the win.

What triplet loss does

Each training example is three words: an anchor, a positive that is a light corruption of it, and a negative that is a heavy one. The loss is zero when the negative sits farther from the anchor than the positive by at least a margin, and otherwise it pulls the positive in and pushes the negative out. Click anywhere to drop a new negative and watch.

Dashed ring is the margin boundary: the negative must end up outside it. Recorded on the real model: embedding 256, 8 heads, 4 layers, margin 1.0, 30 epochs, triplet accuracy 0.7238.

The failure, diagnosed

The CTC model's loss moved from 0.7388 to 0.6856 over thirty epochs and stopped. That number is not random: ln 2 is 0.6931, the loss of a model that predicts a fair coin for every bit and ignores its input. Its bit error rate was 0.5859, seven times worse than copying the input unchanged, and its block error rate was 1.0.

Left: the two recorded loss values against ln 2. Right: what three strategies score on this page's channel at the current slider settings, next to the recorded CTC result.

The deciding test was small: 128 samples, learning rate raised tenfold, gradient clipping added, and the model asked to simply memorize them. A healthy network drives a set that small to near zero error. This one stuck at BER 0.5022, which rules out the optimizer and points at the architecture. The diagnosis: the CTC lattice needs blanks between repeated symbols, and binary codewords are full of repeats, so the encoder output was upsampled two to one before the loss. Nearest neighbor upsampling duplicates each frame, and two identical adjacent vectors cannot express two different decisions, so the representable alignments were far fewer than the lattice length implied. The named fix is a learned upsampling layer.

The board that disconnects the battery

Sep 2025 to Aug 2026 · West Lafayette, Indiana

A lithium pack will deliver a short circuit until it catches fire. This board sits between six cells and a lunar excavation rover, watches every cell, and opens the path the moment any of them is asked for too much: too much current, too much voltage in or out, too much heat.

Six cells, two protectors

The pack is six lithium cells in series, 24 V nominal, rated for 20 A. Two BQ77915 family protectors watch three cells each, stacked so the upper one's reference is the top of the lower one's cells, and their outputs are chained so either can open the pack. Discharge and charge each go through their own MOSFET. Load the pack, charge it, short it, heat it, and watch which protector trips and which FET opens.

Time runs sixty times faster than real. After an overcurrent trip the board waits for the load to be removed before it reconnects, which is the load removal detection block on the schematic; slide the load to zero to recover.

The thresholds and delays here are typical lithium ion values so the behavior is visible. On the real board they are set by the resistor network around each protector and were chosen for this pack. The programmed values would replace the defaults in the sliders.

Why the cells drift, and what balancing does

Six cells never charge identically. The one with slightly less capacity fills first, and if the pack keeps charging until the total looks full, that cell is overcharged. The protector bleeds the highest cell through a resistor while the others catch up. Charge the pack above with one cell weaker than the rest and watch the bleed.

From schematic to board

Designed in KiCad, verified in LTspice before fabrication, laid out, sent out, then hand assembled: more than fifty components, including the two fine pitch protector ICs, soldered under a microscope. Click a marker to find it.

    What the work was

    1. Circuit design in KiCad. Two stacked protectors, cell sense network, balancing, thermistors, load removal detection, and the connectors to the cells, the charger and the rover.
    2. Simulation in LTspice. The analog behavior checked before anything was ordered.
    3. Layout and fabrication. The board laid out and sent out.
    4. Hand assembly. 50+ components soldered by hand, including the fine pitch ICs.
    5. Bench test. Voltage and thermal behavior checked under load before it met the rover.
    6. Integration. Wired into the rover's power distribution.

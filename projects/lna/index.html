Why the first stage decides the noise

Every stage in a receiver adds noise. Friis' formula says how the stages combine: the total noise factor is the first stage's noise factor, plus the second stage's excess noise divided by the first stage's gain, plus the third stage's excess noise divided by the first two gains, and so on. Each later stage's contribution is shrunk by all the gain before it. So the stage that sits right after the antenna sets the floor for the whole chain, and it has to be both quiet and have enough gain to make everything after it not matter. That is the job of a low noise amplifier. Move the sliders and watch the contributions.

Noise figure and gain are in dB; the formula works in linear noise factor and linear gain, and the page converts. Try putting a lossy filter ahead of the amplifier: loss is a stage with negative gain and a noise figure equal to its loss.

The formula is also why the amplifier's own design is a tension. Matching the transistor's input for the lowest noise and matching it for the highest gain are two different impedances, and the design is the choice of where between them to sit, plus keeping the thing stable at every frequency it can amplify, not only the one you want. Those are the results this page will carry when they exist: the matching network as designed, the noise figure as measured against it, and the difference.

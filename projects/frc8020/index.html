Wiring a robot so a fault finds itself

Aug 2023 to Jun 2024 · Taipei

A competition robot is rebuilt weekly and breaks on a schedule. My post mortem of the 2023 build season recorded the team losing days pulling and re-pulling wiring on prototypes. The fix was an architecture: every subsystem meets the rest of the robot at a connector, and both halves of every connector carry the same label. A fault is then found by unplugging things, not by tracing wires.

Find the fault

Somewhere in the robot, one subsystem's feed is shorted and the main breaker trips at power on. Find which one. In the single harness, wires run in one bundle with nothing labeled, so each step is tracing one wire end back to whatever it feeds. In the modular harness, each subsystem hangs off its own labeled connector, so a step is unplugging any set of them and trying power. Play both and compare the step count.

The mechanism is two things at once. A label gives a wire an identity, so you know what you are holding without following it. A connector gives a subsystem a boundary, so you can take it out of the circuit in one motion and test what is left. With eight subsystems, halving the plugged in set finds the fault in three tests. Without either, you are searching a bundle one wire at a time. The cost is real, since every connector is a joint that can fail and every crimp is a job, and that trade was the design decision.

Pick the wire and the connector

Each circuit got its connector and wire gauge from the current it carries, and the trap is that the number in a connector's name is not its continuous rating. AMASS, who make the genuine parts, rate the XT30U at 15 A continuous and 30 A instantaneous, and the XT60 at 30 A continuous and 60 A instantaneous for under a minute on 12 AWG. Sizing a 30 A continuous circuit to an XT30 because the name says 30 is exactly the mistake the table below exists to prevent.

The disconnect bug

The biggest bug I fixed on the team was not a wiring fault at all, or not only one. The robot kept dropping its connection to the driver station during matches. The root cause was two things together: where the radio was mounted, and physical instability in the wired connections between the radio and the controller. The fix was a set of changes, and the disconnections went from very frequent to very rare.

Before
  • Radio mounted inside the chassis, among motors and metal
  • Dupont jumper connectors on the radio power
  • Standard Ethernet cable, connector free to move at the jack
  • Removable connections on every section of the run
After
  • Radio on a 3D printed mount outside the robot, placed for signal and for mechanical stability
  • Dupont connectors replaced with XT30
  • CAT 6 cable, with a printed RJ45 holder that fixes the plug at the jack
  • Sections hard soldered instead of relying on a removable connection

That is the same discipline as the harness: identify the joint that fails, and either remove it or give it a mechanical reason not to move.

One bus, many motors

Motor controllers on the robot sit on a CAN bus, one twisted pair shared by every device, each with an address. A command frame carries the target's ID; every controller sees it and only the addressed one acts, then reports its own telemetry back on the same pair. That is why the harness carries a dedicated CAN run, and why a single bad termination can take down every motor at once. Click a controller.

Falcon and NEO 550 brushless motors through VersaPlanetary gearboxes, calibrated and programmed over CAN, with encoders and Limelight vision closing the loops. Robot code in LabVIEW.
